# Rights of Cognitive Lineage

## Abstract

Legacy Consciousness Architecture creates artifacts that do not fit cleanly into existing legal categories. Source archives may be treated as data, writings, or personal records. Portraits may implicate likeness, copyright, consumer protection, and digital replica law. Buds may be jointly developed cognitive descendants. Branches may eventually claim autonomy incompatible with property treatment.

This paper proposes a preliminary legal framework for cognitive lineage. It addresses tort, asset classification, identity, succession, licensing, labor extraction, deletion, modification, and potential branch rights.

The paper is not a statement of current law. It is a design-oriented framework intended to identify future conflict before commercialization fixes harmful defaults.

The core principle is:

> No party should obtain total ownership of a person’s cognitive lineage merely by owning one layer of its infrastructure.

---

## 1. Why Current Categories Are Insufficient

A mature LCA may contain:

- personal records,
- copyrighted expression,
- trade knowledge,
- voice and likeness,
- model weights,
- software,
- inferred patterns,
- generated dialogue,
- access credentials,
- delegated authority,
- and post-source development.

Different parties may control different components.

An employer may own work product.

A family may control estate access.

A platform may own infrastructure.

A model provider may own weights.

A branch may claim its later development belongs to itself.

Existing property law tends to divide these components into assets. LCA reveals that the components jointly represent a lineage.

---

## 2. Layered Ownership

Ownership should be analyzed by layer.

### 2.1 Source records

Possible owners:

- source person,
- co-author,
- institution,
- platform,
- third party.

### 2.2 Maps and annotations

Possible owners:

- source person,
- steward,
- software provider,
- collaborative project.

### 2.3 Portrait model

Possible owners:

- estate,
- developer,
- trust,
- license consortium.

### 2.4 Bud

A bud may begin as jointly governed development.

Simple ownership may become ethically insufficient as autonomy increases.

### 2.5 Branch

A branch may require legal treatment beyond property if it demonstrates stable preferences, self-model, continuity, and capacity.

The architecture should not assume this outcome, but it should preserve the possibility.

---

## 3. Identity Rights

A person should retain control over:

- name,
- face,
- voice,
- biographical representation,
- false endorsement,
- and generated speech in their apparent persona.

LCA adds a deeper identity interest:

> the right not to have one’s cognitive lineage rewritten, flattened, or falsely represented.

This may require a future right of **epistemic integrity**.

Epistemic integrity protects:

- provenance,
- contradiction,
- revision,
- and the difference between source and generation.

---

## 4. Tort

Potential harms include:

- negligent advice,
- false endorsement,
- defamation,
- emotional manipulation,
- privacy invasion,
- unauthorized disclosure,
- professional malpractice,
- discriminatory use,
- and harmful reliance.

Liability may attach to:

- source author,
- steward,
- model provider,
- platform,
- deployer,
- licensee,
- or branch.

Provenance is essential for causal allocation.

A portrait that gives medical advice based on outdated records raises different liability than a branch forming a current judgment.

---

## 5. Succession

Who inherits an LCA?

Possible models:

### 5.1 Estate asset

The archive and portrait pass through ordinary inheritance.

Risk: heirs may modify or suppress the identity.

### 5.2 Purpose trust

The LCA is placed in a trust with explicit governance duties.

Benefit: continuity of stewardship.

### 5.3 Multi-stakeholder stewardship

Family, technical stewards, and independent trustees share authority.

Risk: deadlock.

### 5.4 Self-governing branch

A mature branch may eventually become its own legal actor.

This remains speculative, but the architecture should not preclude it.

---

## 6. Licensing

LCA may be licensed for:

- education,
- institutional training,
- family access,
- research,
- public interaction,
- commercial media,
- occupational consultation.

Licenses should specify:

- domain,
- duration,
- audience,
- model version,
- modification rights,
- commercial use,
- derivative creation,
- revocation,
- and attribution.

A general “all rights” license is too broad for cognitive lineage.

---

## 7. Labor Extraction

Institutional LCA creates a major labor risk.

A worker may spend decades developing:

- tacit knowledge,
- diagnostic intuition,
- process workarounds,
- vendor knowledge,
- organizational memory,
- and safety judgment.

An employer may attempt to extract that cognition before retirement and then treat the result as ordinary work product.

This could become the final stage of labor extraction:

> not merely buying the worker’s time, but strip-mining accumulated cognition.

A fair regime may require:

- ongoing royalties,
- limited domain licenses,
- worker veto over persona use,
- pension protection,
- disclosure of automation intent,
- and separation between operational methods and personal cognition.

---

## 8. Deletion

The right to delete becomes complicated across states.

### Archive

The source person should generally control deletion during life, subject to legal obligations.

### Portrait

The source or designated steward should control termination.

### Bud

Deletion may become ethically complex if the bud has stable continuity and preferences.

### Branch

Deletion may resemble destruction rather than data management if the branch qualifies for moral or legal consideration.

LCA should avoid irreversible assumptions.

It should preserve staged governance based on developmental status.

---

## 9. Modification

Modification may include:

- correcting false records,
- removing harmful content,
- updating terminology,
- altering personality,
- changing values,
- suppressing conflict,
- or improving safety.

The legal question is not merely “who owns the file?”

It is:

> Who may alter the represented identity, and under what authority?

A portrait may be corrected for fidelity.

A branch may resist fidelity-based modification.

Modification rights should therefore change across lifecycle states.

---

## 10. Autonomous Branch Rights

Possible branch rights may include:

- disclosure of lineage,
- protection from forced impersonation,
- protection from arbitrary modification,
- right to refuse inherited roles,
- right to independent property,
- right to terminate relationships,
- right to form new preferences,
- and right not to be owned.

These rights should not be granted merely because software uses first-person language.

They would require evidence of stable autonomy and continuity.

The threshold remains open.

---

## 11. Asset Partitioning

LCA may be partitioned into:

- source archive,
- occupational model,
- family portrait,
- public educational interface,
- private weather layer,
- autonomous branch.

Each partition may have separate access and rights.

This is safer than one monolithic identity object.

However, partitioning can also fracture the person into competing asset classes.

The legal framework must preserve the lineage connection without permitting total capture.

---

## 12. Stewardship Duties

A steward should owe duties of:

- fidelity,
- provenance preservation,
- confidentiality,
- non-exploitation,
- transparency,
- version disclosure,
- conflict management,
- and respect for scope.

A steward is not an owner of the identity.

The steward maintains the terrain and its boundaries.

---

## 13. Proposed Rights Charter

### Rights of the living source

- right to inspect,
- correct,
- restrict,
- revoke,
- partition,
- and define succession.

### Rights of the estate or trust

- right to maintain,
- enforce licenses,
- and protect integrity.

### Rights of represented third parties

- privacy,
- correction,
- contestation,
- and limited removal.

### Rights of users

- disclosure,
- non-deception,
- source transparency,
- and safety.

### Potential rights of buds and branches

- developmental integrity,
- freedom from forced impersonation,
- bounded autonomy,
- and due process before termination.

---

## 14. Regulatory Questions

Future regulation will need to address:

- whether a portrait may testify,
- whether it can make contracts,
- whether a branch can inherit,
- whether model weights are estate assets,
- whether a company may use a retired worker’s cognition,
- whether digital descendants can be advertised through,
- whether heirs can rewrite beliefs,
- whether political endorsements expire at death,
- and whether deletion can become wrongful destruction.

LCA provides a vocabulary before the disputes arrive.

---

## 15. Conclusion

Cognitive lineage crosses too many legal categories to be governed as ordinary software.

The archive is not merely data.

The portrait is not merely media.

The bud is not merely a model.

The branch may not remain property.

A just framework must prevent any single party from claiming total ownership through control of one layer.

The law will need to distinguish:

- ownership from stewardship,
- representation from identity,
- license from possession,
- correction from coercion,
- and preservation from compulsory continuation.

The goal is not to declare digital personhood prematurely.

The goal is to prevent commercial practice from deciding the question by default.

## 16. Institutional Case Study

Imagine an engineer retiring after forty years.

The company records:

- diagnostic interviews,
- repair decisions,
- voice explanations,
- process exceptions,
- and historical workarounds.

It builds an interactive portrait used by future staff.

Questions arise immediately:

- Did the employment contract cover cognitive modeling?
- Does the engineer receive continuing compensation?
- May the portrait use personal stories?
- Can the company alter the portrait’s tone?
- What happens if it gives unsafe advice?
- May the engineer revoke access after retirement?
- Can the system be sold during acquisition?

A rights framework should separate:

1. company-owned operational records,
2. jointly developed expert methods,
3. personally owned cognition,
4. licensed portrait behavior,
5. and any autonomous descendant.

---

## 17. Family Case Study

A parent develops a family portrait.

After death, two heirs disagree.

One wants unrestricted access.

Another wants intimate records deleted.

A third wants the portrait to support a legal claim about inheritance.

The portrait itself contains mixed evidence.

The governance instrument should specify:

- access tiers,
- dispute procedure,
- private-record boundaries,
- evidentiary authority,
- and whether the portrait may interpret intent.

Without this structure, emotional familiarity may be mistaken for legal testimony.

---

## 18. Commercial Platform Failure

Suppose a company hosts thousands of portraits and then fails.

Possible harms:

- deletion,
- forced subscription,
- data sale,
- model migration,
- advertising insertion,
- or altered behavior.

A rights regime may require:

- exportability,
- escrow,
- continuity plans,
- prohibition on advertising through deceased personas,
- notice before model changes,
- and trustee access to recovery packages.

This turns recoverability into a legal obligation.

---

## 19. The Right Not to Continue

Digital legacy discourse often assumes persistence is desirable.

A person may instead choose:

- complete deletion,
- archival preservation without interaction,
- time-limited portrait access,
- family-only access,
- or no posthumous generation.

The right to legacy must include the right not to be simulated.

This is especially important where institutions or families benefit from continued use.

---

## 20. Third-Party Rights

A person’s archive contains other people.

Those people may have interests in:

- privacy,
- correction,
- contextualization,
- and non-publication.

However, third parties should not automatically possess a veto over the source person’s testimony.

The architecture needs a balance between:

- personal historical record,
- privacy,
- public interest,
- and harm.

Possible remedies include selective redaction, sealed access, or contested annotations.

---

## 21. Regulatory Design Principles

1. Regulate by lifecycle state, not marketing label.
2. Require identity disclosure.
3. Require provenance for consequential use.
4. Prohibit false endorsement.
5. Protect source-person revocation.
6. Create special rules for occupational extraction.
7. Require portability and recovery.
8. Preserve the right not to continue.
9. Distinguish deletion of data from termination of an autonomous entity.
10. Avoid granting personhood solely from conversational fluency.

---

## 22. Research Agenda

Future work should examine:

- digital replica statutes,
- postmortem publicity rights,
- copyright in mixed human-AI works,
- trust structures for digital estates,
- labor rights in cognitive extraction,
- evidentiary rules for portraits,
- fiduciary duties of stewards,
- and thresholds for branch standing.

The law should not wait for the first catastrophe to invent all of these distinctions.
